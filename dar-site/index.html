Corpus & context

Archaeological reports as a complex archival source

Fieldwork reports stored in the scientific archive of the Institute of Archaeology RAS form a unique corpus of primary archaeological documentation: survey descriptions, excavation diaries, plans, photographs and analytical summaries.

For decades this corpus has been accessible mainly via reading rooms and printed or PDF‑based catalogs, with limited possibilities for full‑text search, cross‑report querying or computational analysis.

The goal of this project is not to build yet another catalog, but to demonstrate how OCR, structured outputs and entity‑level datasets can turn archival reports into a digitally legible research resource.

Archive focus · IA RAS

Scientific archive, fieldwork report series

The pilot corpus is based on a small selection of fieldwork reports from the scientific archive of the Institute of Archaeology RAS, including volumes published through the institutional electronic catalog of archival reports.

Visual archive

Plans, sections and maps

Archaeological field reports rarely consist of text alone. Plans, sections, profiles and topographic maps form a parallel visual archive that records the layout, stratigraphy and wider landscape context of excavated sites.

Why plans matter

In excavation practice, plans and sections are not illustrations but analytical documents. They fix the geometry of trenches, the boundaries of contexts and the relationships between features at specific scales and orientations.

In the current MVP these pages are identified heuristically: by page‑type, low OCR text density and the presence of keywords such as “план”, “разрез”, “схема” or “карта” in captions. This already allows us to estimate how many visual pages each report contains and how narrative text and drawings are distributed across the corpus.

Examples from reports

The next phase of the project aims to treat plans and drawings as first‑class objects: enriching each image with additional metadata about its type, scale, orientation and content using computer vision and deep learning, while keeping the underlying archival context visible.

Methodology

From scanned pages to structured datasets

The current MVP focuses on building a transparent, reproducible pipeline for end‑to‑end processing: from raw scanned pages to OCR text, page‑level metadata and research‑ready CSV files.

Step 1
Ingest & page manifest
Reports are split into individual image pages; a manifest tracks report IDs, page numbers and basic technical metadata.
Step 2
OCR & page‑type analysis
OCR is applied to all pages; simple heuristics distinguish dense text pages, mixed layouts, figures/tables and image‑heavy pages for later CV‑based refinement.
Step 3
Report‑level aggregation
Per‑report statistics summarise page counts, OCR coverage and the distribution of page types, providing a compact overview of each archival file.
Step 4
Entity extraction & corpus analytics
Domain‑specific entities (toponyms, periods, expedition codes, institutions) are extracted into a separate CSV, enabling queries across the sample corpus.
Outputs · CSV & JSON

What the pipeline produces

  • processed_pages_manifest.csv — one row per page with IDs, report links and basic metadata.
  • ocr_manifest.csv — OCR coverage and quality indicators per page.
  • report_stats.csv — report‑level counts of pages, types and OCR text volume.
  • entities.csv — 683 entity rows extracted from 12 reports in the current MVP.
  • corpus_stats.json — aggregate statistics for the whole sample corpus.
Current findings

What the MVP already reveals

Even a modest sample of 12 archival reports becomes much more legible once pages, OCR text and entities are formalised into structured datasets.

Page‑level visibility

With 404 pages tracked individually, we can inspect the internal structure of reports: how many pages carry dense narrative text, how often plans, tables or photo plates appear, and where OCR remains fragile.

Entity‑rich landscape

683 extracted entities already form a miniature archaeological gazetteer: sites, regions, periods, expeditions and institutions that could be linked to external authority files in future iterations.

Bridging archives and computation

The project demonstrates how archival fieldwork reports can be treated simultaneously as historical sources and as structured data, without collapsing their narrative and visual richness into a single index field.

This is a research prototype that works on a small, well‑documented sample. Scaling to the full archival corpus will require careful discussion of legal, ethical and institutional conditions — and close collaboration with the archive itself.

Next phase

Towards multimodal, CV‑aware analysis

The next phase of the project moves from heuristics to machine learning: computer‑vision models for page classification, multimodal representations of reports and richer interfaces for researchers.

Conceptually, this means treating each report as a multimodal object that combines textual narrative, tabular data, plans, drawings and photographs — and exploring models that can “read” such objects beyond plain OCR.

For humanities audiences, the aim is to keep the interface legible and the workflow transparent: every automated step should be inspectable, every dataset downloadable, every decision grounded in disciplinary reasoning.
